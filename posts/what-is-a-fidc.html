What is a FIDC

A FIDC is a pool of resources: a fund whose assets are made up of receivables, such as trade receivables (duplicatas) and credit card receivables.

Basic terms

Instrument (título)

Term used to represent a receivable. It can be:

  • invoices (notas fiscais);
  • contracts;
  • card Receivable Units (UR);
  • other financial instruments.

Batch (remessa)

In our system, a batch is a set of instruments that have not been traded yet. Each package has a unique identifier called idRemessa, which lets our trading desk find it quickly by number.

These sets can be grouped or split as needed, either at the request of the trading desk or of the client, which gives flexibility in managing the batches.

Key participants

  • Assignor / Issuer (cedente/emissor) — the entity that issues the instrument.
  • Drawee / Debtor (sacado/devedor) — the entity responsible for paying the instrument.

Credit rights

These are the receivables purchased by the fund: trade receivables (duplicatas), credit card receivables, Bank Credit Notes (CCBs), contracts, and so on.

FIDC structure

Quotas: the classes define the priority order for receiving returns and amortization.

Class Priority Risk Who usually holds it
Senior first to be paid lower external investors
Mezzanine intermediate medium investors / structurers
Subordinated last to be paid, first to lose higher usually the assignor / originator

PDD

An allowance for doubtful accounts, which adjusts the fund's net assets. On a redemption, if a receivable is past due it is adjusted by the PDD: its value goes down and the investor loses money.

Subordination ratio

The amount of loss that can happen before the senior investor is affected. For example:

  • fund value: R$ 100.00;
  • senior investor: R$ 80.00;
  • junior (subordinated) investor: R$ 20.00, or 20% of the fund's value.

In other words, if the fund loses up to 20% of its net asset value, the loss is borne by the junior investors alone.

Receivables advance (adiantamento de recebíveis)

This is what the fund actually does: it buys a receivable that would only be paid in the future and pays the assignor now, minus a discount (deságio). The money life cycle below is that operation, step by step.

Money life cycle (overview)

  1. origination, by the assignor (cedente);
  2. assignment (cessão) of the receivable to the fund;
  3. validation of the backing (lastro) and of eligibility;
  4. it enters the fund's portfolio (estoque do fundo);
  5. settlement (liquidação): the drawee pays;
  6. it becomes cash.

Credit card advance (adiantamento de cartão de crédito)

When a purchase is split into installments, one Receivable Unit (UR) is created for each installment date, in the name of the assignor.

To advance that money, the fund goes through the following steps:

  1. it queries the schedules (agendas) at the registrars — B3, Núclea and CERC;
  2. it finds the URs for each date;
  3. it signs a contract that transfers ownership of the receivable (troca de titularidade);
  4. from then on the acquirer (credenciadora, the card machine company), which would pay the assignor after 30 days, pays the fund instead;
  5. in exchange, the fund pays the assignor right away, discounted by the deságio.

So the difference from a trade receivable is mostly the backing and where it lives: instead of an invoice against a drawee, the fund holds URs registered at a registrar, and the payer is the acquirer.
